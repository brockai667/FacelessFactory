## 035 Snake Bite
OK
- 81,410–137,880 people die from snakebite each year; about three times as many are left with amputations or permanent disability. WHO fact sheet https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/snakebite-envenoming (OK-number: "up to 138,000 a year")
- Do NOT: suck out venom, cut the wound, apply a tourniquet, apply ice or soak it in water. CDC/NIOSH https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/topics/snakes/symptoms.html ; WHO treatment page https://www.who.int/teams/control-of-neglected-tropical-diseases/snakebite-envenoming/treatment
- Suction removes almost nothing: in a human model about 0.04 % of mock venom (Alberts et al., Ann Emerg Med 2004, PubMed 14747805) [snippet; say "almost none", number may be used as "less than one percent"]
- Do: get emergency care / hospital, antivenom is the effective treatment (WHO); keep calm and still, immobilize/splint the limb (WHO), "neutral position of comfort" (CDC); remove rings and watches before swelling starts (CDC); do not drive yourself (CDC).
- Venom mainly spreads through the lymph system, which is why moving spreads it (immobilisation rationale).
AVOID: "keep the bite below the heart" (not in CDC/WHO); "tie it off"; "bandage it tightly" (only for some Australian snakes); any number for how fast movement spreads venom.

## 036 Falling Elevator
OK
- Jumping at the last moment barely helps: best human jump ≈ 7 mph; from 165 ft the impact drops from about 72 mph to 65 mph; from 33 ft from 31 to 25 mph. Physicist Chris Baird, West Texas A&M https://wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2012/12/05/if-im-on-an-elevator-that-breaks-loose-and-plummets-down-the-shaft-can-i-avoid-harm-by-jumping-at-the-last-second/ (and you cannot time it)
- Betty Lou Oliver, elevator operator, survived a 75-storey (≈1,000 ft) elevator fall in the Empire State Building on 28 July 1945 after a plane hit the building and cut the cables; Guinness record for the longest fall survived in an elevator; air trapped in the shaft and the coiled cables under the car cushioned it; she broke her back, neck and pelvis. Guinness https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2023/10/How-an-Elevator-attendant-Survived-a-1-000-Ft-Fall-Down-the-Empire-State-Building-759670 ; NYC Municipal Archives https://www.archives.nyc/blog/2019/10/18/the-empire-state-plane-crash-july-28-1945
- Elevators hang on several steel ropes and have automatic safety brakes (Otis safety brake, 1854); a true free fall is extremely rare [industry sources, snippet: say it without a number].
AVOID: "lying flat is recommended" (no authority recommends it; allowed only as "some physicists suggest lying flat to spread the force, it is unproven"); "jumping does nothing" (say "barely helps"); any statistic on elevator falls.

## 037 Rip Current
OK
- A rip current is a strong, narrow flow of water away from shore. It carries you out, it does not pull you under. NWS https://www.weather.gov/news/211309-7ripcurrent
- Usually 1–2 feet per second, measured up to 8 feet per second, faster than an Olympic swimmer. NWS https://www.weather.gov/safety/ripcurrent-science (OK-number: "up to 8 feet per second")
- Swimming straight back against it exhausts even strong swimmers (NWS).
- Rips cause about 82 % of surf-beach lifeguard rescues and an estimated 100+ US drownings a year (USLA estimate; Brewster et al., NHESS 2019 https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/19/389/2019/ ; NWS counts ~60).
- Do: stay calm, don't fight it; float to save energy; swim parallel to the shore (sideways) until out of it, then back at an angle; if you can't, float and wave/yell for help. NWS https://www.weather.gov/safety/beach-ripcurrents-hazards
- Research (MacMahan; McCarroll 2014): rips often circulate, floating can bring you back; neither float nor swim-parallel works every time, so "float first, then swim sideways" is the honest order.
AVOID: "pulls you under"; "8 feet per second" as the typical speed; "flip, float, follow" attributed to NOAA.

## 038 Night In Antarctica (alcohol in the cold)
OK
- Alcohol widens blood vessels in the skin: it gives a sensation of warmth but increases heat loss. CDC MMWR, Hypothermia-Related Deaths 2003 (mm5308a2)
- Alcohol is a listed risk factor for hypothermia; it impairs judgment and coordination (CDC; Wilderness Medical Society guideline via AAFP https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/1101/p571.html)
- Shivering is how the body makes heat; it weakens around 32 °C core temperature and stops near 30 °C (WMS/AAFP). Larger doses of alcohol impair shivering (Kalant & Lê 1983, PubMed 6144116).
- What keeps you alive: insulation incl. from the ground, head and neck covered; stay dry and out of the wind; eat and drink warm, high-calorie food (WMS/AAFP).
AVOID: any "drops your temperature by X degrees" (studies show small, dose-dependent effects); "alcohol stops shivering" without "can / in larger amounts"; St. Bernard brandy barrel as a proven fact (only "the brandy barrel is a legend from a painting", weakly sourced: better leave out).

## 039 Into A Black Hole
OK
- Tidal forces stretch a falling object; NASA calls it spaghettification. https://science.nasa.gov/universe/what-happens-when-something-gets-too-close-to-a-black-hole/
- At a supermassive black hole of 4.3 million Suns (like Sagittarius A* in the centre of our galaxy) you cross the event horizon first; destruction by spaghettification follows 12.8 seconds later. NASA Goddard visualization (Schnittman) https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/supermassive-black-holes/new-nasa-black-hole-visualization-takes-viewers-beyond-the-brink/ (OK-number: 12.8 seconds)
- Small (stellar-mass) black holes have stronger tides and rip objects apart before they reach the horizon; for an 8-solar-mass hole lethal tides start about 400 km out while the horizon is ~24 km. NASA-hosted black hole FAQ https://science.nasa.gov/apod/frequently-asked-questions-faqs-on-black-holes (say "hundreds of kilometres out")
- To someone watching from far away you never quite get there: you slow, redden and fade (NASA Goddard page; FAQ).
- Past the horizon nothing, not even light, gets back out. A black hole does not suck things in from far away. https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/
AVOID: "cross the horizon intact and fine"; "frozen forever on the horizon" (image fades quickly); a number for a 10-solar-mass hole.

## 040 Holding Your Breath
OK
- The urge to breathe is driven mainly by rising carbon dioxide, not by running out of oxygen. Pernett, Schagatay & Holmström, Frontiers in Physiology 2024/25 https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1515232/full
- Untrained people (81 non-divers) gave up after about 60–80 seconds with blood oxygen still around 92 % (same paper). (OK-number: "about 92 percent")
- The involuntary contractions of the diaphragm are the body's CO2 alarm (physiological breaking point).
- Hyperventilating first lowers CO2, delays the urge and raises the risk of blacking out, underwater without warning. DAN https://dan.org/alert-diver/article/hypoxia-in-breath-hold-diving/ ; US Naval Safety Command "Shallow Water Blackout"
- Record static apnea on air: 11 min 35 s, Stéphane Mifsud, 2009 (Guinness / AIDA). With pure oxygen beforehand: 29 min 3 s, Vitomir Maričić, 2025 (Guinness). Never mix the two.
- Safe viewer moment: hold for 10–15 s sitting on dry land only. Never in water, never after hyperventilating, never as a game alone.
AVOID: "oxygen is at 98 %"; "you have plenty of oxygen for minutes"; inviting a long hold.

## 041 Pulling 9 G
OK
- High positive G pulls blood away from the head; the brain runs short of oxygen. FAA/CAMI report DOT/FAA/AM-23/6 (Copeland & Whinnery 2023) https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/66373/dot_66373_DS1.pdf
- Order with slow onset: gray-out (loss of colour and side vision), black-out (no vision but awake), then G-LOC (loss of consciousness). With very fast onset there is no warning (same report).
- Relaxed, unprotected person: vision dims around 3–4 G, blackout near 5 G (Air & Space Forces Magazine; Whinnery) [snippet; say "around 4 or 5 G"]
- G-LOC: about 12 seconds fully unconscious, then roughly 15 more seconds confused (Whinnery & Whinnery 1990, 501 centrifuge episodes). (OK-number: "about 12 seconds")
- Pilots fight it with the anti-G straining manoeuvre (tense legs and abdomen, short forced breaths) and a G-suit that squeezes the legs (FAA).
- At 9 G everything weighs nine times as much (physics; a 5 kg head weighs like 45 kg).
AVOID: "untrained people pass out at 4–6 G" as a sourced fact; "tunnel vision" as an official stage name is fine as plain description; "G-suit gives you 9 G".

## 042 Falling Without A Parachute
OK
- Vesna Vulović survived a fall of 10,160 m (33,333 ft) on 26 January 1972: Guinness record for the highest fall survived without a parachute. She was pinned inside the tail section, which landed on a snowy slope. Guinness https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2022/4/how-vesna-vulovic-survived-the-highest-fall-ever-with-no-parachute-697786.html
- A falling body stops speeding up when air resistance equals weight (terminal velocity): belly-down roughly 120 mph / 195 km/h, reached after roughly 10–12 seconds; head-down is much faster. (Textbook physics, e.g. OpenStax University Physics vol. 1 ch. 6.4: TO BE RE-CHECKED against the source before render; write "about 120 miles an hour".)
- There is no tested procedure. Case reports point to: landing feet first, on snow, trees or a slope, which spread the stop over more time.
AVOID: presenting survival tips as proven; "157 survivors"; implying Vulović fell as a free body.

## 043 Car Sinking In Water (reserve)
OK
- About 400 people die in submerged vehicles in North America each year. McDonald & Giesbrecht, Aviat Space Environ Med 2013;84:498-510 (OK-number)
- A car floats only about 15 to 63 seconds before water reaches the bottom of the side windows ("about a minute"). IAED "Get Out Fast" https://iaedjournal.org/get-out-fast ; same paper.
- Waiting for the cabin to fill so the pressure equalizes lowers your chance: by then the car is deep and you have one breath (2013 paper). Water outside holds the door shut.
- Protocol (Dr. Gordon Giesbrecht, University of Manitoba): Seatbelts off, Windows open, Out, Children first. Do not phone first.
- Electric windows should still work if used quickly; side windows can be broken (tempered), windshields are laminated and cannot (newer cars may have laminated side glass too).
AVOID: "30–120 seconds"; "wait and open the door" as a plan.

## 044 Jellyfish Sting (reserve)
OK
- Urine does not help; in lab tests on Portuguese man o' war it made stinging cells fire. Fresh water did too. Wilcox et al., Toxins 2017 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5450697
- Rinse with sea water, not fresh water; remove tentacles (not with bare fingers). Healthdirect (Australian government) https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/jellyfish-stings
- Hot water, about 45 °C for about 20 minutes, relieves pain better than ice. Wilcox & Yanagihara, Toxins 2016 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4848624 ; Cochrane review CD009688; Healthdirect.
- Vinegar: standard for tropical box jellyfish in Australia; for bluebottle / man o' war guidance says avoid it. So no blanket "use vinegar".
- A box jellyfish sting can kill within minutes; call emergency services for tropical stings (Healthdirect).
AVOID: "vinegar for all jellyfish"; "150 million stings a year" on screen (secondary); "tweezers" as an official instruction (say "lift the tentacles off, not with bare fingers").

## 045 Tornado (reserve)
OK
- Highway overpasses are not shelters: wind speeds are typically higher there and you are a stationary target for flying debris; on 3 May 1999 in Oklahoma three people were killed under or near overpasses. NWS Norman https://www.weather.gov/oun/safety-overpass
- Strongest tornado wind ever measured: about 300 mph (NSSL gives 318 mph), by mobile Doppler radar, Bridge Creek–Moore, Oklahoma, 3 May 1999. https://www.nssl.noaa.gov/education/svrwx101/tornadoes/faq/ (OK-number: "about 300 miles an hour")
- Do: basement or safe room, else an interior room away from windows; cover head and neck. A car and a mobile home are not safe. If caught in a car: stay buckled, head down below the windows, or lie in a spot clearly lower than the road. NWS https://www.weather.gov/safety/tornado-during ; https://www.ready.gov/tornadoes
AVOID: "opening windows" myth as NWS-debunked (no primary source found); warning lead time numbers; "many died under overpasses".

## 046 Earthquake (reserve)
OK
- Do not stand in a doorway: in modern homes doorways are no stronger than the rest of the house. Do not run outside or to another room during shaking. The "triangle of life" is rejected as dangerous. Earthquake Country Alliance https://www.earthquakecountry.org/dropcoverholdon/
- Do: Drop onto hands and knees, Cover head and neck (under a sturdy table if one is near), Hold on until the shaking stops. In bed: stay there, face down, cover your head with a pillow. Ready.gov https://www.ready.gov/earthquakes
- Most injuries come from falling and flying objects and from people trying to move during the shaking (McBride et al., USGS-led study) [snippet: say "most injuries", no number].
AVOID: "doorways were safe only in old adobe houses" (unsourced); Northridge injury numbers.
